# Episode 1, script v4: cold listener notes

*I listened once to the narration only, at about 150 words a minute. I skipped the drawing cues, the "Suggested for Carl" boxes and the word-count table, and opened no other file first. The listener is a working parent who goes to church but isn't a theologian, hearing it once while the drawings go up. Checklist: "Cold listener" in `.claude/skills/story-script/references/editors.md`.*

## 1. What I felt, section by section

- **Opening (channel intro).** Waiting. Twelve seconds of the channel name and a web address before anything happens, and my thumb is on the skip button. "This is Understanding Kingdom" sounded slightly odd, but I took it for the show's name.
- **1. The coin.** Hooked within a sentence. "It was most likely a Tuesday" told me I was in a story with a careful storyteller. "On any other matter, they would not have stood together" made me curious. The trap was crystal clear by "If He said no, He was calling for revolt, and those soldiers were watching." Turning the coin over felt like being handed it. It sags a little while the soldiers are explained, and the scene ends twice. This is the best three minutes of the episode.
- **2. What belongs to God?** The story stops and a teacher steps in front of the drawing. "Let him have it back" explained something I had already understood. Then "Most of us miss it, because we have forgotten what they knew" hooked me again, because I wanted to know what I'd missed. But I couldn't yet see how "what a kingdom actually was" answers "what belongs to God?". I took that on trust.
- **3. A word that lost its weight.** A second of "where are we now?" at "Back at the start". "Herod ruled Galilee, and Rome ruled Herod" was crisp. Then I smiled in recognition at the castle and the dragon, and laughed guiltily at "We usually say it quickly, on the way to daily bread." I got the two-lines point first time. It felt like a good Sunday-school lesson: a lecture, but a likeable one.
- **4. The farmer who forgot his king.** Back in a story, and the present tense pulled me right in. "He sent his face" gave me a small chill. As a parent, "That year he brings in the harvest alone" got me. "Behind the wall his sons helped to build" was a lovely click. "He could forget the king only because the king was doing his job" stuck. "We live a lot like that farmer" landed: work, kids, whole seasons without a thought of God. Then the voting paragraph lost me, with a second argument, a political aside, a psalm, and "That includes the farmer", which I didn't follow. It finished strongly with "No stone marks the edge of God's land."
- **5. The king's face.** A quick history link. "Remember that coin in the temple courts?" pulled me back, and "lived in two kingdoms at once" made me think of myself. But the same question, word for word, came a second time with no answer, and I got a bit impatient: just tell me.
- **6. The first page.** "Go back with me", then Genesis 1, which I know, and I tuned out for the land-and-plants part. Then the penny dropped hard (see "The moment I leaned in most"). Hearing Jesus' answer again, it meant something completely different this time. "You are the statue that breathes, and the coin that walks" is a great line, but "statue" tripped me for a second, because the whole way through it had been a stone.
- **7. What the image gives you.** Tender. The grandmother who no longer remembers her own name put a lump in my throat. But it was over almost before it started, and it felt like a new idea arriving just after the big moment. It also left me briefly unsure: am I meant to give myself to God, or have I nothing to hand over?
- **8. The question comes home.** The quietest, most intimate part. The man on the road looking at his own hand gave me a shiver. "Now hold out your own hand": I would actually do it (if I was alone). The card-tap trigger is concrete. "The one field in your life" stayed abstract for me.
- **9. Next time.** The spell broke. "Treaty" sounds like school history, and "we will lay the treaty beside the Bible" sounds like homework. I was mildly curious about hearing "I will be your God" the way Israel first heard it, but not hooked.
- **Closing (channel outro).** Warm, but "send Carl a message" made me wonder who Carl is, and "Jesus plus nothing" puzzled me (see "What I did not understand").

## 2. The moment I leaned in most

"Last of all, God does something the farmer would have understood. He places His image in His land."

For about five minutes I had been carrying two things, the coin and the farmer's stone. In that sentence they became one thing: I am the stone. Then "Caesar could stamp his face on silver. God had stamped His on them." closed it.

Close behind: "Then he looks at the hand that is holding it." (section 8), and "That year he brings in the harvest alone." (section 4).

## 3. Where my attention drifted (with the sentence just before each)

Worst first.

1. **Before the story starts.** Just before: "This is Understanding Kingdom, exploring the Kingdom of God through Scripture, covenant and the life of faith." Then a web address. Nothing has happened yet, and this is where a YouTube viewer skips or leaves.
2. **The voting paragraph.** Just before: "All that time, God has been keeping watch over us." Then "We also forget Him because of the world we grew up in. We vote our leaders in, and I am grateful we can." The story's spell broke into an argument. "I am grateful we can" sounded like the narrator heading off a complaint. I drifted until "No stone marks the edge of God's land."
3. **The next-time teaser.** Just before: "Then his seal is pressed into the clay." Then "After that, we will lay the treaty beside the Bible." This is where I'd close the video.
4. **Straight after the coin scene ends.** Just before: "Then they left Him and went away." Then "The first half of His answer was simple." The scene was over and nothing new had started, so it felt like the end of the video. I was only pulled back by "Most of us miss it, because we have forgotten what they knew."
5. **The jump to Galilee.** Just before: "They knew what a kingdom actually was." Then "Back at the start, when Jesus began to preach, John the Baptist was in prison." I had been promised an answer and got a time jump and a prison.
6. **Genesis I already know.** Just before: "And God said, 'Let there be light,' and there was light." Then "Day by day He fills His world…". I tuned out for about fifteen seconds, until "God does something the farmer would have understood."
7. **The question asked a second time.** Just before: "And in the temple they sang that God is 'the great King over all the earth.'" Then the same question again, word for word. More impatience than drift.
8. **Mild sags.**
   - Just before: "That day, Roman soldiers stood along the colonnades, watching the crowd." Then the fortress back-story, at the very moment I wanted Jesus' answer.
   - Just before: "In front of all those people, the men who had set the trap could not catch Him out." The scene had already ended, and then it ended again ("Astonished by His answer, they fell silent…").

Overall, I had to hold "what belonged to God?" for roughly six minutes before it was answered. The farmer carried me through, but sections 3 and 5 felt like waiting.

## 4. What I did not understand on one hearing

- **"Jesus knew their hypocrisy."** What was hypocritical? Was it the flattery, or that they had the coin on them? It was never said.
- **"Tiberius Caesar, son of the divine Augustus."** I thought Caesar *was* his name. Who are Tiberius and Augustus? The next paragraph saved the meaning, but the names went straight past me.
- **"Back at the start."** The start of what? It cleared up a second later.
- **"King Herod had put him there."** Is that the Christmas Herod? Is he connected to the Herodians? It didn't matter, but it snagged.
- **The farmer.** I couldn't tell whether he was a Bible character, a real person from history or a made-up example. I assumed made-up, and it didn't bother me.
- **"All along, he has held that field under the king."** I roughly got "held under" (the field was never really his), but it's a legal phrase.
- **"All who live in it. That includes the farmer."** I didn't see why the farmer is singled out. Is it because he lives under a different king? It went right past me.
- **"You are the statue that breathes."** What statue? It was a stone.
- **"Remember the one field in your life you still think of as your own."** I knew it was a picture, but not what mine is. Money? Time? Career? My kids? There was no example to hang it on.
- **"Send Carl a message."** Who is Carl? Is he the man who has been saying "I believe" and "I often think"? If so, why does he talk about himself by name?
- **"Remember, Jesus plus nothing."** I took it as "Jesus is enough". But straight after "Bring it to God", I wasn't sure whether it meant I don't need to do anything.

## 5. Details that felt like decoration rather than meaning

- **"They had come down from their fortress at the corner of the temple courts, as they did at every festival."** Where they came from doesn't matter. That they are watching does.
- **"John the Baptist was in prison. King Herod had put him there."** I didn't know why I was told this. If the point is "this king locks up prophets", it wasn't said.
- **"…and they curse anyone who harms the stone."** Vivid, but it never matters again.
- **"Tiberius Caesar, son of the divine Augustus" and "Pontifex Maximus".** The Latin and the names are museum-label flavour. The two claims would only mean something if they came back (see "Loose threads").
- **"and I am grateful we can."** An aside that protects the narrator. It does nothing for me.
- **"Land rises out of the waters. Plants cover it, and the sky and the seas fill with living things."** Only "All of it is His, because He made it." did any work for me.
- **Borderline: "and when his father dies, he buries him in that ground."** It makes the field feel like his, which matters, but nothing comes of it at the turn.

Not decoration; please keep: "most likely a Tuesday", the dragon, and "The whole trap was small enough to fit in the palm of a hand."

## 6. Loose threads

- **The coin's two claims.** "That small piece of silver made two claims. Caesar was the son of a god. And Caesar stood between his people and heaven." That sounded like it was going to matter. As a churchgoer I was waiting for "and the real Son of God was the one holding the coin". It never came, and neither claim is mentioned again. This is the biggest loose thread.
- **The hypocrisy.** It is named but never shown. Section 5's "lived in two kingdoms at once… they sang that God is 'the great King over all the earth'" half-explains it, but it isn't tied back.
- **"And this was the feast of freedom from a foreign king."** A strong line that seemed to promise something about freedom, and about which king they really served. The Passover is never mentioned again after section 1.
- **"Some men who had been sent."** Sent by whom? I never found out.
- **"The kingdom of God has come near. Repent and believe the good news!"** Neither "come near" nor "repent" is picked up. I assumed that's for a later episode.
- **The farmer's king and God.** The farmer's king took his grain and his boys ("Nobody asks the farmer whether he agrees"), and then God is compared to that king. For three minutes I had a small "so is God the one who takes my kids?" aftertaste. Section 7 answers it sideways ("before you had handed Him anything at all"), but nothing joins the two, so it heard like a new thought rather than an answer.
- **Who "I" is.** I heard "I believe", "I want you to", "I am grateful" and "I often think", then "send Carl a message". I never learned who was talking to me.
- **Small ones:** the curse on the stone, and the father's grave.

What paid off, and I noticed:
- The trap "small enough to fit in the palm of a hand" came back as the hand on the road, and then my own hand.
- "On the way to daily bread" came back as "slowly this time".
- The wall the sons built came back as the wall that saved the family.
- "Hold onto that question" and "knew the answer straight away" were both answered in section 6.

## 7. What I'll remember tomorrow (my own words)

Caesar's face was on the coin, so the coin was his. God's face is on me, so I'm His: I'm the coin that walks.

There was a farmer who walked past the king's face on a stone every day and forgot the king, until the enemy came and the king's soldiers were on the wall. We forget God because He's quietly doing His job.

"Your kingdom come" and "your will be done" are the same prayer said twice, and I rattle it off.

Look at your hand: whose image is it? Think about it when you tap your card.

What I won't remember: Tiberius, Augustus, the Latin, the Herodians, which psalm it was, the voting point, the days of creation, or the name of next week's episode (just "something about a treaty").

## 8. Did the ending make me want to do something?

Yes, a little, and more than most church videos do.
- I'd hold my hand out there and then.
- I'd probably pray the Lord's Prayer more slowly next Sunday.
- Thinking of God when I tap my card is a good hook because I do it every day. It might last a few days.

But I wouldn't actually "bring my field to God", because I didn't know what my field was. And the teaser and outro walked straight over the moment: the last things in my ear were a web address and a slogan, not "Your will be done."

## 9. Audiobook, sermon or lecture?

It is a story-sermon: two strong audiobook stories, joined by teaching, ending in something like an altar call.

- **Audiobook:**
  - From "It was most likely a Tuesday" to "Then they left Him and went away", with a slight dip at "Caesar was the Roman emperor, and Rome's armies had conquered this land."
  - Again from "Long before Caesar…" to "He could forget the king only because the king was doing his job."
  - Again for the four sentences starting "I often think about those men walking home that evening".
- **It turns into lecture or teaching at:**
  - "The first half of His answer was simple."
  - all of section 3, peaking at "They ask for one thing, in two ways."
  - "Later, rulers found a way to send their face even further."
  - "Go back with me to the first page of the Bible."
  - the teaser's "we will lay the treaty beside the Bible."
- **It turns into a sermon at:**
  - "I want you to hold onto that question."
  - "We live a lot like that farmer."
  - "You carry it too."
  - all of section 7
  - "Now hold out your own hand…" and "This week, every time you pay for something…"

The best blend is section 6, where the teaching *is* the story's payoff. The narrator's "I" mostly makes it feel like a preacher talking, not an audiobook narrator. The exception is "I often think…", which felt like a storyteller.

## 10. Score

**7/10 for "this reads like an audiobook".** Sections 1 and 4 on their own are a 9. What pulls it toward a sermon or a class:
- the middle (sections 2, 3 and 5)
- the voting paragraph
- the last minute: homework, then the teaser, then the outro.
