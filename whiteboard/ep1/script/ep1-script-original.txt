Jerusalem, a few days before Passover, the great spring festival of the Jewish people. The courtyards of the temple, the centre of Jewish worship, are crowded with pilgrims. A teacher from Galilee named Jesus is sitting among them, and people have gathered to listen.

A group of men make their way to the front. They call Him Teacher, and they tell Him they know He is an honest man who teaches the way of God truthfully. Then they ask their question. Is it right to pay taxes to Caesar, or not?

Caesar was the Roman emperor. Rome had conquered the Jewish homeland, its soldiers kept order in Jerusalem, and the people groaned under the occupation. Every tax they paid reminded them who ruled them. So everyone listening could see the trap. If Jesus said yes, He would lose the crowd. If He said no, He was calling for a tax revolt, and the Roman soldiers were a short walk away.

Jesus asked to see the coin used for the tax. Someone handed Him a denarius, a small Roman silver coin, worn smooth by many hands. He held it up.

On one side was the head of the emperor of the day, Tiberius. The words around it called him son of the divine Augustus, the emperor before him, whom Rome honoured as a god. The other side called Tiberius highest priest. In one small piece of metal, a man claimed to rule the world and to stand between people and heaven.

"Whose image is this?" Jesus asked. "And whose inscription?"

"Caesar's," they said.

"Then give back to Caesar what is Caesar's," He said, "and to God what is God's."

The men who had come to trap Him were amazed. They left Him and went away.

The coin carried Caesar's face, so it belonged to Caesar, and he could have it back. That leads to a second question. Whose face do you carry? Every man in that courtyard had learned the first page of the Scriptures as a boy. "So God created mankind in His own image; in the image of God He created them." Christians since the earliest days of the church have heard that verse behind Jesus' words. If the coin with Caesar's image belongs to Caesar, then the person with God's image belongs to God. That means you, every part of you, on every day of the week. You are the currency of a different kingdom, and the King's face is on you.

The Kingdom of God was the first thing Jesus announced when He began to preach. "The time has come," He said. "The Kingdom of God has come near. Repent and believe the good news!" To repent means to turn around and change direction. The crowds who heard Him knew exactly what a kingdom was. They lived in one, under Rome.

For us, a kingdom is harder to picture. Most of us grew up in democracies, where power starts with the people. We vote leaders in, and when we are unhappy we vote them out. In the world of the Bible, power flowed the other way. Authority came down from above, from the king, and his decree simply became the way things were.

That difference changes how we hear the Bible. We read the word law and picture a rule that a parliament passed and a later vote could change. We read covenant and picture a contract between two equal sides who bargained over the terms. We read kingdom and picture a country we could move away from. Each of those pictures belongs to our world, and each one misses what the words meant to the people who first heard them.

So let us step into their world.

In a museum in Istanbul there is an old, weathered stone, carved with symbols and writing. It is a boundary stone. Stones like it once stood beside the road at the edge of a king's land. Every traveller who passed one knew what it meant. From this stone onward, this king's law applies. His officials collect the taxes here, his soldiers keep the peace, and his protection covers everyone on this side of the line.

Three things stood behind that stone. The first was the king himself. Nobody elected him. His authority came down to him through his family line, and inside his borders his word was final. The second was his land, real ground with real edges, where his word was law. The third was his people. They were bound to him by a solemn agreement called a covenant. The king offered it on his own terms, and his people received it. Loyalty to the king was the one condition of belonging, and going over to a rival king was treason.

Hold those three together, a king, his land and his people, and you start to hear the Bible differently. Again and again God says to His people, "I will be your God, and you will be My people." It sounds tender, and it is. It is also the formal language of covenant, the words by which a king bound a people to himself. In the laws God gave to Israel, the ancient Jewish nation, He says plainly, "The land is Mine." With those words God sets His own boundary stone. Elsewhere He says the whole earth is His. And His Kingdom reaches further than any border, because it is His reign wherever His word is obeyed. When people asked Jesus when the Kingdom of God would come, He told them it was already in their midst.

The direction of power matters most of all. God chose to make Himself known inside that ancient picture of a king. When He came to Israel, He came as the Great King. He chose them, rescued them from slavery in Egypt, and bound them to Himself. The one question put to them was whether they would keep faith with Him. Absolute rule from above belongs to God alone, and so the Bible places even Israel's own kings under His law.

Now think of a farmer in ancient Assyria, one of the great empires of that world. He gets up before dawn and works a field he calls his own. He sells his grain, raises his sons and buries his father in that ground. He has never seen the palace, and he goes for years without giving the king a thought. As far as he can tell, he is his own man.

Then one harvest time the king's officials arrive to claim a share of his crop, and there is no arguing with them. Another year the king is building a wall, and the farmer's sons are called away to work on it. And one day an enemy army appears at the border. Now the farmer understands why he has paid and worked all these years. The king's army is all that stands between his family and slaughter. His field was held under the king the whole time.

Most of us live like that farmer. We get up, go to work, raise our children and run our own lives, and we can go whole seasons without a thought for God. We take His patience to mean He is absent.

The Bible calls God the King of kings. To our ears that sounds like high praise, the way we might call someone the champion of champions. In the ancient world it was an exact title. It belonged to an emperor who ruled over other kings, and those lesser kings served him. When the Bible calls God the King of kings, it places Him above every ruler on earth. He is the Great King. The crowd in the temple courts knew what a king was. Many of us have forgotten.

That farmer in Assyria never saw his king. So how did the king's will reach his village? A king sat in one palace, and his word had to travel to the edges of his land. There were four ways it travelled, and each one has its match in the Bible.

The first was the royal decree. When a king declared something, his word made it happen. On the first page of the Bible, God speaks in exactly that way. "And God said, 'Let there be light,' and there was light." Later, at Mount Sinai in the desert, after God had brought Israel out of Egypt, the Bible says, "God spoke all these words," and His commands became law the moment He spoke them.

The second was the messenger. A king sent heralds to speak for him, and when the herald spoke, the king was speaking. To welcome the herald was to welcome the king. The prophets of the Bible were God's heralds, and they opened their messages the way a royal envoy did. "Thus says the Lord," meaning, these are the King's words, and I am only His mouth. One day the prophet Nathan walked into the palace of King David, Israel's most famous king. David had taken another man's wife and arranged for her husband to be killed in battle. Nathan was an ordinary man and David was the king, yet David had to listen, because through Nathan the Great King was speaking. Jesus carried this forward when He sent out His followers. He told them, "Whoever listens to you listens to Me; whoever rejects you rejects Me; but whoever rejects Me rejects Him who sent Me."

The third was the written covenant. Spoken words fade, so a king had his covenant written down and kept. God did the same, and He gave an instruction that every seven years, at the Feast of Tabernacles, the autumn harvest festival, the whole law should be read aloud. Men, women and children gathered to hear it, along with the foreigners living among them. A people that stops hearing its covenant soon forgets whose it is.

The fourth was the royal image, and it matters most. Kings set up statues of themselves and carved their likeness on stone monuments, in town squares and in the cities they conquered. In later centuries they stamped their faces on coins. The image made the king present in places he would never visit, and it told everyone that this place was his.

Now listen to the first page of the Bible again. God has made the world, and it belongs to Him. Then He says, "Let Us make mankind in Our image, in Our likeness," and He gives them the task of ruling over His world. The Great King places His own image in His land, and this image is alive. You are the statue that breathes and the coin that walks.

That gives every person a worth no one can take from them. Your value was stamped into you before you had done anything at all. A newborn baby carries the King's image in full. So does a grandmother with dementia who no longer remembers her own name, and so does a prisoner the whole world has written off.

It also gives you a responsibility. In the ancient world a king's image was treated with great care, because it stood for the king himself. Your body, your mind and your gifts carry the King's likeness, and how you treat them is how you treat Him.

All four ways run in one direction, down from the throne to the people. The King speaks, He sends His messengers, He writes down His covenant, and He places His image in His land. Our part is to hear, to receive, to remember, and to live as what we already are.

Some of the men in the temple courts must have gone home that evening with Jesus' question still turning in their minds. Whose image is this? He had asked it about a coin, and the same question reaches each of us today.

So this week, each time you hold money in your hand, remember whose image you carry. Let God have your working week as well as your Sunday. And look at every face you meet as someone who carries His image too.
