# Episode 1, v3: story editor's notes

*Story editor pass on `ep1-script-v3.md`, read against `story-bible.md`, the story editor checklist in `editors.md`, and `craft.md`. Times are at 2.5 words a second from the start of the intro. They fall within a few seconds of the script's own table.*

**In one line.** The coin scene and the farmer read like an audiobook. Most of the rest of the middle, and the two minutes after the answer, still read like a lesson with pictures. By my rough count about 43% of the narration is scene; the bible says 70%. After the coin scene it falls to about a third.

**Protect in the next draft:**
- All of section 1.
- The farmer's first five beats (e01–e05).
- Holding the answer back to ~9:50.
- "Caesar could stamp his face on silver. God had stamped His on them."
- "You are the statue that breathes, and the coin that walks."
- The hand at i02.

## (a) The single biggest structural change

**Make the middle one story, the farmer's, in place of a traveller, a farmer and a lecture about stones.**

From about 3:30 to 9:00 the episode works through a list of points about what a kingdom is, with a picture for each:

- A kingdom is where the king's will is done (c04).
- It has edges (section 4).
- Everything in it is held under the king (section 5).
- Power comes down from the throne (e08).
- The king sends his face (section 6).

Only the farmer has a person, stakes and a turn, so give the other points to him.

- **The traveller's stone becomes the farmer's stone.** It stands at the edge of his land, carved with the king's face. He has never seen the king, but he has walked past that face all his life.
  - This replaces three things: the faceless traveller (d01–d02), the Cyprus stone told from the museum (f01–f02), and the hinge "So how did a king make his claim felt in places he would never visit?"
  - The d01 cue already draws the border stone with a king's figure. The accuracy editor should confirm that such a stone fits a farmer near Assyria's border.
- **The enemy army gathers on the far side of that stone (e05).** "One step past that stone" now has his family behind it, not a passing traveller.
- **His turn is learning what the carved face meant all along.** f02's "This place was his now, and so were the people in it" becomes his realisation instead of a museum label. Carve the usual royal curse into the stone, against anyone who defaces it, to plant h01–h02 (notes 1 and 2).
- **Bring him back at the peak.**
  - In g03, "something every person in that ancient world would have understood" becomes something that farmer would have understood.
  - In g04, "The kings of that world carved their likeness in stone" becomes his king and his stone. All his life he walked past his king's face, carrying the image of a greater King without knowing it.
  - The climax then lands on someone the listener has followed for three minutes.

What stays and what you gain:
- f03's coins and f04's "Remember that coin?" stay as the road back to the temple.
- Most of the art carries over. d01's stone becomes his, and f02's close-up of the carved face is what he looks at when the army comes.
- The listener makes two fewer jumps in place and time.
- The episode's key plant, the image, sits inside a scene the listener cares about.
- The episode should come out close to a minute shorter.

## (b) The emotional peak, and whether it is earned

**Where it is:** about 9:50–10:15. First "Caesar could stamp his face on silver. God had stamped His on them." (b02), then "You are the statue that breathes, and the coin that walks." (g04).

**Earned in logic, half earned in feeling.** The coin's face and claim (a08–a09), the empty coin (p01) and the king's face in stone (f01–f02) set it up properly, and holding the answer back is the right call. Three things stop it landing in feeling:

- **It lands on "them", the questioners.** They left at 2:50 and were never people to us (note 5 fixes that). The farmer is the one person the listener has come to care about, and he has no part in it.
- **Worth isn't planted.** Worth is what makes the claim something to be glad of, and it arrives after the peak as a thesis ("This is where your worth comes from"). So the most moving picture in the script, "a grandmother who no longer remembers her own name", is spent inside a lecture paragraph.
- **The point lands six times in under two minutes.** Between b02 and the hand it lands at b02, g04, b03, h01, h02 and i01. The peak is worn down before the listener gets there.

**The fix.** Make the hand at ~11:35 the peak: "Hold out your hand for a moment, and look at it. Whose image is this?" It is the refrain's last use, it pays off the palm from a07, and it is the only moment the listener does something while listening.
- Keep b02 and g04.
- Cut the explaining after them (notes 1, 2 and 4).
- Plant worth in the farmer (note 1).
- Let the man with the coin look at his own hand just before the listener does (note 5).

## Notes, most important first

**1. h01.** "This is where your worth comes from. It was stamped into you before you had done a single thing."
- **Problem:** Worth is half the one meaning, and the reason to be glad of the claim, but nothing plants it, so it arrives after the peak as a thesis.
- **Do:** Plant it in the farmer. Let the officials count what he is worth to the throne: his grain on the threshing floor, his sons for the wall.
  - h01 then pays it off by contrast, e.g. "The farmer's king counted what he could hand over. God's image was on him before he had handed over anything." Go from there straight to the newborn.
  - This also fixes a risk. As written, the listener's only picture of being claimed is Assyria, the empire that carried Israel into exile, taking grain and sons while "Nobody asks the farmer whether he agrees." The contrast is what turns "claimed" into "glad".

**2. h02.** "The King's image also lays a responsibility on you. In the ancient world, how you treated a king's image was how you treated the king himself. That goes for the image you carry, and for the same image on every face you meet."
- **Problem:** New history brought in after the climax reads as a lecture, and "every face you meet" is a second invitation competing with j01.
- **Do:** Plant the fact in the stone scene: royal stones carried curses on anyone who damaged them, which is the bible's own source for h02.
  - Then let h01's last picture, "the prisoner the whole world has written off", carry it, and cut "The King's image also lays a responsibility on you."
  - "Your calling" is currently three different asks: g03's "so that they may rule", this beat, and j01. Send the listener home with j01's.

**3. e09.** "All who live in it. That includes the farmer, and it includes you."
- **Problem:** It answers "what belongs to God?" at ~7:35, two and a half minutes before the climax, and b03 says it again.
- **Do:** Stop at "That includes the farmer." The listener hears "and me" without being told, and the first "you" is saved for g04. With (a), this is also where the stone pays off a second time: no stone marks the edge of God's land.

**4. b03.** "Now hear Jesus' answer again. Give back to Caesar what is Caesar's, the coin with his face on it. And to God what is God's, the person who carries His. The tax question was small. The ownership question was total. It means you, every part of you, on every day of the week."
- **Problem:** It explains a payoff the listener got twenty seconds earlier (craft §9: "Explaining a metaphor right after it lands").
- **Do:** Both halves of Jesus' sentence are now full, so let it stand without the glosses.
  - Keep the author's "small / total" pair if he wants it.
  - Cut "It means you, every part of you, on every day of the week." j01 can show "every day" and "every part" as pictures instead (notes 6 and 19).

**5. i01.** "They had come to trap a teacher with a coin. Now His question was going home with them. It had been easy to answer about the coin. It was much harder to answer about themselves."
- **Problem:** The last scene is a summary that ends on a neat pair, while the drawing (a man looking at the coin in his palm) does the storytelling.
- **Do:** Narrate the drawing. One of them opens his hand, looks at Caesar's face, then at his own hand. Stop there and turn to the listener. To earn it, make him one man we can follow:
  - He took the coin "from his own purse" (a07).
  - Jesus gives it back to him (b01), so "Let him have it back" becomes an action. i01's drawing already assumes he has it.
  - He is one of the boys who learned the first page (g01).
  - a07's "small enough to fit in the palm of a hand" then pays off at i02.

**6. j01.** "Then think of the one part of your life you have been keeping for yourself, and pray over it the words Jesus taught. 'Your kingdom come. Your will be done.'"
- **Problem:** Two plants are owed a payoff here and neither can be heard: the farmer's "field he thinks of as his own" (e01) and the prayer said "quickly, on the way to daily bread" (c03).
- **Do:** Use their words: "Then think of the one field in your life you still think of as your own, and pray over it, slowly this time: 'Your kingdom come. Your will be done.'"

**7. e08.** "Part of the reason we forget Him is the world we grew up in. Most of us live in democracies, where power starts with the people. We vote our leaders in, and we can vote them out. I am grateful for that. In the farmer's world, power ran the other way. It came down from the throne."
- **Problem:** A civics lesson with no scene sits between the farmer and his payoff. Section 3 and "Nobody asks the farmer whether he agrees" have already done its job.
- **Do:** Keep the author's gratitude in one line and go straight to his best sentence: "We vote our leaders in, and I am grateful we can. Nobody ever voted God in." Then the psalm. The ballot-box board can go.

**8. e07.** "Whole seasons can go by without God crossing our minds. We take His patience to mean that He is absent."
- **Problem:** The farmer forgot his king because the king was quietly doing his job. "Patience" swaps in a reckoning held off, and it leans toward judgement just as the story has earned gratitude.
- **Do:** Keep the parallel exact and let the listener make it: "We can forget Him for the same reason the farmer forgot his king."

**9. d01–d02, if you don't take (a).** "In the world of the Bible, a king's land had edges, and stones marked them. Some of those stones survive today, still carrying the names of the kings who set them up." / "Everyone who passed a stone like that knew what it meant. From here on, this king's law runs. His officials collect the taxes here, and his soldiers keep the peace. His protection covers everyone on this side of the line."
- **Problem:** There is no person, no stakes and no turn, only a list of what a kingdom does, which the farmer shows a minute later with his grain, his sons and his family.
- **Do:** Give the stone to the farmer. If the traveller stays, open on the road with the stone in sight, and give him a reason to care which side of it he is on.

**10. f01–f02, if you don't take (a).** "That farmer never saw his king. So how did a king make his claim felt in places he would never visit? He sent his face." / "One of those stones was found on the island of Cyprus, far across the sea from the king's palace. It still carries his carved likeness."
- **Problem:** The rhetorical-question hinge doesn't follow, because the farmer lives in Assyria, not a far-off land, and the stone is then told from the museum instead of from the city it stood over.
- **Do:** Tell the day the stone went up, from the people of that city. Their own kings have bowed, and now the Assyrian king's face looks out over their roofs: "This place was his now, and so were the people in it." Where it was found belongs in the video description.

**11. Two habits that turn scene into explanation.**
- *The general sentence:* "Caesar was the emperor in Rome." / "At festivals they came out and stood along the colonnades, watching the crowd." / "Every tax the people paid reminded them who ruled them." / "Everyone who passed a stone like that knew what it meant." / "When an Assyrian king brought a far-off land under his rule, he had his own likeness carved in stone."
- *Stepping out to the museum:* "Some of those stones survive today…" / "One of those stones was found on the island of Cyprus…"
- **Problem:** "Every…", "at festivals…" and "everyone…" are a textbook talking, and "survive today" and "was found" break the spell to prove a point.
- **Do:** Tell it about this day, this man and this stone, and keep the proof for the bible and the video description. At a05, start from what is in the courts and say whose it is second: "That day, Roman soldiers stood along the colonnades, watching the crowd. They were Caesar's men, and Caesar's armies had taken this land."

**12. a06 → a11.** "If Jesus said yes, He would lose the crowd. If He said no, He was calling for revolt, and those soldiers were watching." … "And they were amazed at Him. They left Him and went away."
- **Problem:** The fork is set up well but never shown breaking, so the listener never sees the trap close on the trappers, which is the turn the bible names for this section.
- **Do:** Add one beat after "Give back…" in which neither the crowd nor the soldiers have anything to take hold of.
  - Luke has the line: "They were unable to trap him in what he had said there in public. And astonished by his answer, they became silent." (Luke 20:26, NIV; accuracy editor to confirm.)
  - p02's "I believe that is what amazed them" then has a plain reason to reach past.

**13. b02.** "So whose face do you carry? Every man in those courts knew the answer from the first page of his Scriptures. Caesar could stamp his face on silver. God had stamped His on them."
- **Problem:** The question is put to "you" and answered about "them" in one breath, and it uses up the listener's question two minutes before the hand.
- **Do:** Keep this beat on the men ("So whose face did they carry?"), and let g04's "You are the statue that breathes" be where it turns to the listener. The b02 board reads "Whose face do you carry?", so changing it is the owner's call on existing art.

**14. p01 → f04.** "If the coin with Caesar's face belonged to Caesar, what belonged to God?"
- **Problem:** Nobody asks the question aloud between ~3:10 and the answer at ~9:50. Sections 3–6 keep it alive in theme only, and a listener can't hear theme.
- **Do:** Ask it once more, in the same words, right after "Remember that coin in the temple courts?" (f04), so Genesis answers a question the listener is holding.

**15. c01.** "When Jesus first began to preach in Galilee, this is what He said." … "Nobody in that crowd needed the word kingdom explained."
- **Problem:** Section 3 has no scene with stakes, so the weight of "kingdom" is explained instead of felt.
- **Do:** Mark gives you the stakes in the verse before: "After John was put in prison, Jesus went into Galilee, proclaiming the good news of God" (Mark 1:14, NIV). Open there. Galilee's own ruler, Herod, has just locked John up, and Jesus walks into Galilee and says "kingdom" out loud.

**16. a04.** "Some of them were Pharisees, who resented Rome's rule. The others were Herodians, men who were comfortable with Roman power."
- **Problem:** These are two dictionary entries. The story is that rivals had joined forces for one question, so each side of the trap had its own witness, and the bible says the author always gives that why.
- **Do:** Add one plain line: "On any other day, they would not have stood together."

**17. a02.** "Jerusalem was full for the Passover, the feast that remembered how God had brought His people out of slavery in Egypt."
- **Problem:** This gloss never pays off, though it is the scene's hidden stake: a feast of freedom from a foreign king, kept under a foreign king's soldiers.
- **Do:** Cash it at a05, where the soldiers come out "at festivals". That is why they were watching (Josephus, *Jewish War* 5.244, already in the bible). Otherwise trim it to "Jerusalem was full for the Passover."

**18. a08–a09.** "Augustus was the emperor before Tiberius, and Rome had declared him a god." / "On one side was a man's face…" / "On the other side, the coin called Tiberius Pontifex Maximus, the highest priest."
- **Problem:** There's a history footnote in mid-scene, and the coin is described like a catalogue entry while Jesus stands holding it.
- **Do:** Keep the camera on Jesus' hands. He looks at the face, then turns the coin over.
  - Cut "Augustus was the emperor before Tiberius". a09's "a man claimed to be the son of a god" already explains "divine".
  - The high-priest claim either earns one clause (it was made in the courts where Israel's own high priest served) or goes.

**19. j01.** "Every time money passes through your hand, let the face on it ask you that question."
- **Problem:** Most listeners now pay by card or phone, and many coins carry no face, so the daily trigger will rarely fire.
- **Do:** Make the trigger the act of paying, which keeps Caesar in it: "Every time you pay for something this week, ask that question again." f03's board already shows coins passing from hand to hand at a stall, so the plant is in place.

**20. e01.** "Now imagine a farmer who lives inside that line, in the ancient kingdom of Assyria."
- **Problem:** "Now imagine" announces the scene from outside it (craft's "Picture this" and "let's step into their world").
- **Do:** Begin inside it: "Before dawn, a farmer in Assyria leads his ox out to a field he thinks of as his own." The present tense and "a farmer" already tell the listener he is a picture, not a record.

**21. a03.** "Jesus was there too, as He had been each day that week."
- **Problem:** The hero walks on as an afterthought ("there too"), and the beat stands still between setting the trap and springing it.
- **Do:** Let the men find Him, so the trap keeps moving toward its target: "They found Jesus where He had been every day that week, sitting in the courts, teaching."

**22. k01.** "Next time, we will open the kind of document a great king used to bind a people to himself, and lay it beside the Bible."
- **Problem:** The tease describes a document in lecture terms when it could hook with a moment.
- **Do:** Tease a scene from the k01 board: a seal pressed into clay as a people hear what they are swearing to. Then God's line and the title.

**23. The bible's question 3: reading the title in the intro.** "They knew what a kingdom actually was."
- **View:** Yes, read "Episode one: What a Kingdom Actually Was" at the end of the channel intro. An audiobook names its chapter, and p02's line then lands as the title arriving.

## Checklist answers

- **One meaning:** It is clear in the bible. The ending lands the claim (the hand, and the part you've kept back) but not the worth, which section 8 spends first (notes 1, 2 and 6).
- **Dramatic question:** It is set by 0:30 ("a question sharpened into a trap") and stated at ~3:10. After that nobody asks it aloud until ~9:50, and Psalm 24 answers it early at ~7:35 (notes 3 and 14).
- **Beats that neither move the story nor deepen the meaning:**
  - d01–d02
  - e08
  - the museum half of f02
  - the restatement in b03
  - the opening sentence of h02
- **Sections without a real scene** (people, place, stakes, a turn): sections 3, 4 and 8. Sections 2 and 10 are bridges. Sections 6 and 9 have only the outline of a scene (notes 10 and 5).
- **Where telling replaces showing:**
  - "Caesar was the emperor in Rome."
  - "In the world of the Bible, a king's land had edges, and stones marked them."
  - "Part of the reason we forget Him is the world we grew up in."
  - "So how did a king make his claim felt in places he would never visit?"
  - "This is where your worth comes from."
  - "The King's image also lays a responsibility on you."
- **Plants and payoffs:** The coin's face and claim pay off well at b02. Still owed:
  - the palm (a07 → i02)
  - the field (e01 → j01)
  - the quick prayer (c03 → j01)
  - the crowd-and-soldiers fork (a06 → a10)
  - the Passover (a02)

  Worth and the dignity of the image (h01–h02) have no plant at all.
