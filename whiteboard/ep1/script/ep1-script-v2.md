# A Faith Driven Life — Episode 1: What a Kingdom Actually Was

*Narration script, v2. Section times are rough guides for the existing scene plan.*

## A — The coin (0:00–1:38)

It's a few days before Passover, and Jerusalem is packed. The temple courts are hot and loud with pilgrims, traders calling out, families looking for somewhere to stand, and in one corner a teacher from Galilee named Jesus is sitting with a crowd around Him.

A small group pushes its way to the front, and it's an odd pairing: some Pharisees, who resented Rome, and some Herodians, who did very nicely out of it. Men like that only stand together when they want the same thing, and today they want Jesus trapped. "Is it lawful to pay taxes to Caesar, or not?"

Everyone listening knew there was no safe answer. Rome had conquered the land, Roman soldiers watched the temple courts from the fortress next door, and every tax payment was a reminder of who was in charge. If Jesus said yes, He'd lose the crowd. If He said no, He'd be calling for revolt.

So He asks for a coin. Someone presses a denarius into His hand, a little silver disc worth a day's wages, worn smooth by a thousand fingers. He turns it to the light. There's the profile of the emperor Tiberius, and around the edge the words *Tiberius Caesar, son of the divine Augustus.* One small piece of metal, and a man is claiming to be the son of a god.

Jesus holds it up. "Whose image is this? And whose inscription?"

"Caesar's," they say.

"Then give back to Caesar what is Caesar's," He says, "and to God what is God's."

They're amazed, and they walk away. But He's left a second question hanging in the air, one they couldn't shake off. If the thing stamped with Caesar's image belongs to Caesar, what about the thing stamped with God's?

## B — The Kingdom is near (1:38–3:33)

Every man in that courtyard had known since boyhood the first page of the Scriptures: "So God created mankind in His own image" (Genesis 1:27). In their world an image marked ownership. The coin could go back to Caesar, but the person holding it belonged, all of them, to someone else.

That was the heart of what Jesus preached. From the very first day He'd been saying, "The Kingdom of God has come near. Repent and believe the good news" (Mark 1:15). And when He said *kingdom*, nobody needed it explained, because they were living in one.

For most of us it's much harder. We grew up in democracies, where power starts with the people and flows upward. In the world of the Bible it ran the other way. Authority came down from the king, and what he decreed simply became the way things were.

That one difference quietly changes how we read the Bible. We see the word *law* and imagine something a parliament could repeal next year. We see *covenant* and picture two equals haggling over a contract. We see *kingdom* and think of a country we could leave if we got fed up. None of those is what the first hearers heard.

So let's step into their world, starting at the edge of a road. In a museum in Istanbul there's a slab of dark, weathered stone carved with symbols and writing. It's an ancient boundary stone from Mesopotamia. Stones like it marked where a king's land began, and every traveller who passed one understood the message: from here on, this king's law applies. His officials collected the taxes here, and his protection covered everyone on this side of the line.

Which raises a question. What exactly stood behind that stone?

## C — King, land, people (3:33–5:10)

Three things, really. First, the king himself. Nobody elected him, and inside his borders his word was final. Second, the land, real ground with real edges, where his word was law.

Third, the people, and this is the part we've lost. They were bound to their king by a covenant. Historians have found many of these treaties from the ancient Near East, in which a great king, the suzerain, bound a smaller king and his people to himself. The great king set the terms. Loyalty was the one condition of belonging, and going over to a rival king was treason.

Now listen to how God speaks. Again and again He tells Israel, "I will be your God, you will be My people." It's tender, and it's meant to be, but it's also the formal language of covenant. In Leviticus 25:23 God says, "The land is Mine," and with that He sets His own boundary stone. The psalmist pushes the border to the edges of the world: "The earth is the Lord's" (Psalm 24:1). And when people asked Jesus when the Kingdom would come, He told them it was already in their midst (Luke 17:21).

So when God came to Israel, He came the way a great king comes. He rescued them from slavery in Egypt, brought them to Sinai, and offered His covenant. The only question was whether they'd keep faith. And here's a telling detail. When Israel later had kings of its own, Deuteronomy 17 required each one to write out his own copy of God's law and read it all his life, so he'd never think himself above his brothers. Even the king had a King.

Yet most ordinary people hardly thought about their king at all.

## D — The farmer who forgot (5:10–6:44)

Picture a farmer in ancient Assyria. He's up before dawn, breath steaming in the cold, walking out to a field he thinks of as his. He's never seen the palace, and whole years go by without him giving the king a thought.

Then one harvest the king's officials arrive and take their share of his crop, and there's no arguing about it. Another year the king is building a wall, and the farmer's sons are called away to carry bricks. Then one spring an enemy army appears on the border, and all at once he understands what he's been paying for, because the king's soldiers are all that stand between his family and the sword. That field was held under the king the whole time. He could forget the king only because the king was doing his job.

Most of us live a lot like that farmer. We go to work, raise our kids, run our own lives, and whole seasons pass without God crossing our minds. We take His patience to mean He's absent.

The Bible has a title for Him that should wake us up. It calls Him the King of kings. To us that sounds like praise, the way you'd call someone the champion of champions, but in the ancient world it was a real title, used by emperors who ruled other kings as vassals. The crowd in the temple courts understood that. Many of us have forgotten.

But if the farmer never saw his king, how did the king's will ever reach his field?

## E — How the King rules (6:44–8:17)

A king's word had to travel, and it travelled in four ways, each with its match in the Bible.

The first was the royal decree. When a king declared something, his word made it so, and on the first page of Scripture God speaks exactly like that: "Let there be light," and there was light. At Sinai, "God spoke all these words" (Exodus 20:1), and they became law as He said them.

The second was the messenger. A king's herald would stand up and begin, "Thus says the king," and from then on the king was speaking. Listen to how the prophets open: "Thus says the Lord." Think of Nathan walking into King David's palace after David had taken another man's wife and arranged for her husband to die. Nathan tells him a story about a rich man who stole a poor man's only lamb, and David is furious. Then Nathan looks at him and says, "You are the man." An ordinary man stood before Israel's greatest king, and the king had to listen, because a greater King was speaking. Jesus carried this on when He sent out His disciples: "Whoever listens to you listens to Me" (Luke 10:16).

The third was the written covenant. Kings had their treaties written down and read out again, and God did the same. Every seven years, at the Feast of Tabernacles, the whole law was read aloud to everyone, men, women, children and the foreigners among them (Deuteronomy 31:10–13). Over and over God tells His people to remember, because a people who stop hearing their covenant soon forget whose they are.

And that brings us to the fourth way, which turns out to be the most personal of all.

## F — The image (8:17–10:29)

Ancient kings set up statues and stone images of themselves in town squares and in the far-off cities they'd conquered. A king couldn't be everywhere, so his image stood in for him and told everyone who passed, "This place is mine."

Now read the first page of the Bible with that in mind. God has made the world, and it all belongs to Him. Then He says, "Let Us make mankind in Our image, in Our likeness." The Great King sets His image in His land, only this image isn't carved from stone. You're the statue that breathes and the coin that walks.

That gives you a worth nobody can take away, because it was stamped into you before you'd done a single thing. A newborn baby carries the King's image in full. So does a grandmother who no longer remembers her own name, and so does the prisoner the world has written off.

It also gives you a responsibility. A king's image was handled with care, because how you treated the image was how you treated the king. Your body, your mind and your gifts carry His likeness.

Notice that all four ways run in one direction, down from the throne to us. The King speaks, He sends, He writes, and He places His image. Our part is to hear, to receive, to remember, and to live as what we already are.

Come back to the temple courts one last time. Those men walked home that evening with a coin in their purse and a question they couldn't put down, and now that question has come all the way to you. Hold out your hand and look at it. Whose image is this?

Here's one thing to do this week. Every time you touch money, a coin, a note, the card you tap at the till, let it remind you whose face you carry, and hand Him one part of your life you've been keeping for yourself. Then look at the next face you see and remember that they carry His image too.

Next time, we'll lay one of those ancient treaties beside the Bible, and you'll never hear the word covenant the same way again. The next episode is called *The Treaty of the Great King.*
