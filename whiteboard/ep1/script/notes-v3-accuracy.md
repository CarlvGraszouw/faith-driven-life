# Accuracy notes — Episode 1, script v3

*Accuracy editor: scripture, history and theology. I read only `ep1-script-v3.md`, `story-bible.md` and the accuracy checklist in `editors.md`. Translation checked: **NIV 2011**. I checked everything from memory, without web access, and I say "unsure" wherever I could not confirm a detail. I checked drawing cues only where their on-screen words quote Scripture or a coin.*

## Summary

- **Scripture: all correct.** Every quotation in the narration matches the NIV 2011 word for word, and every reference in the story bible is right. Where the 2011 NIV differs from the 1984 edition, the script uses the 2011 wording ("the imperial tax", "Whose image", "Give back", "has come near"). The covenant line in k01 is Jeremiah 7:23. Exodus 6:7 and Jeremiah 30:22 word it differently.
- **Fix (3):** "the emperor in Rome… His armies" (note 6); "the first two lines" (22); the question mark inside the on-screen quotation on p01 (17).
- **Soften (12):** the Tuesday (1); "Every tax" (8); "from his own purse" (11); "knew the answer" (18); Galilee "under Rome" (20); "the place where" (24); the border stones (25); what the Cypriots "knew" (33); "every man" and "every person" (36, 39, 41); "in full" (44). Two more are optional: "the same thing twice" (23) and "Most of us live in democracies" (28).
- **History:** sound once those changes are made. The story bible cites kudurrus for the border-stone line, but they don't support it. Swap them for true border stelae (note 25). Smaller source fixes and additions are listed at the end.
- **Theology:** sound and not overclaimed. "The ownership question was total" is well supported, not least by the very next scene in Mark (note 43). Reading "to God what is God's" as the person who bears God's image goes back to Tertullian, but it is still an interpretation. Let "I believe" cover it once, in section 2 (note 18). The script agrees with the author's lines quoted in the story bible. I did not read the book itself.

## Findings, in script order

### 1. The coin

**1. a02.** "It was a Tuesday, in the last week of Jesus' life."
**Verdict: soften.** No Gospel names the day. Tuesday is the traditional reckoning, counting Mark's days from a Sunday entry (Mark 11:11, 12, 20, 27; 14:1, "only two days away"). Some scholars put the entry on a Monday, or the crucifixion on another day, and either moves this scene. The author's own line is hedged: "My best research places the scene on the Tuesday…"
**Corrected:** "It was most likely a Tuesday, in the last week of Jesus' life."

**2. a02.** "…among them were some men who had come with a question sharpened into a trap."
**Verdict: correct**, with an optional precision. Mark 12:13: "Later they sent some of the Pharisees and Herodians to Jesus to catch him in his words." "They" are the chief priests, the teachers of the law and the elders (Mark 11:27; 12:12). Luke 20:20 calls the men spies.
**Corrected (optional):** "…among them were some men who had been sent with a question sharpened into a trap."

**3. a03.** "Jesus was there too, as He had been each day that week. He sat teaching…"
**Verdict: correct.** Luke 21:37; Mark 11:11, 15–17; Matthew 26:55 ("Every day I sat in the temple courts teaching").
**Corrected:** no change.

**4. a04.** "Some of them were Pharisees, who resented Rome's rule. The others were Herodians, men who were comfortable with Roman power."
**Verdict: correct.**
- **Pharisees.** More than 6,000 refused an oath of loyalty to Caesar (Josephus, *Antiquities* 17.41–42). A Pharisee named Saddok co-founded Judas the Galilean's tax revolt of AD 6. That movement agreed with the Pharisees on everything else and held God to be "their only Ruler and Lord" (*Ant.* 18.4, 23). Most Pharisees still paid the tax, so "resented" is right and "resisted" would not be.
- **Herodians.** They appear only in the Gospels (Mark 3:6; 12:13; Matthew 22:16). They were most likely supporters of Herod's family, which ruled by Rome's favour, so "comfortable with Roman power" claims no more than we know.

**Corrected:** no change.

**5. a04.** "Teacher," they said, "we know that you are a man of integrity." … "Is it right to pay the imperial tax to Caesar or not?"
**Verdict: correct.** Mark 12:14, NIV 2011 (the 1984 NIV had "pay taxes to Caesar").
**Corrected:** no change.

**6. a05.** "Caesar was the emperor in Rome. His armies had conquered this land, and his soldiers kept watch over Jerusalem from a fortress at the corner of the temple courts."
**Verdict: fix (small).** There are two slips:
- **Not "in Rome".** Tiberius left Rome in AD 26 and ruled from Capri for the rest of his reign, so in AD 30–33 he was not in Rome.
- **Not his armies.** No emperor conquered Judea. Pompey took it in 63 BC, under the Republic, and Augustus made it a province in AD 6 without a war.

The rest is right. The fortress was the Antonia, at the north-west corner of the temple courts (Josephus, *Jewish War* 5.238). Rome held it in Jesus' day: the high priest's robes were kept there under Roman guard from AD 6 to 36 (*Ant.* 18.90–95).
**Corrected:** "Caesar was the Roman emperor. Rome's armies had conquered this land, and his soldiers kept watch over Jerusalem from a fortress at the corner of the temple courts."

**7. a05.** "At festivals they came out and stood along the colonnades, watching the crowd."
**Verdict: correct.** At every festival the garrison spread out, armed, along the porticoes (*Jewish War* 5.244). At Passover under Cumanus (c. AD 48–52), soldiers stood on the porticoes (*Jewish War* 2.224; *Ant.* 20.106–108). Josephus is describing the AD 40s to 60s, but he calls it the standing custom, so it is fair for AD 30. If you quote Whiston's translation, note that his "legion" in 5.244 is a cohort.
**Corrected:** no change.

**8. a06.** "Every tax the people paid reminded them who ruled them."
**Verdict: soften.** They also paid the temple tax and tithes, and those pointed them to God, not Caesar.
**Corrected:** "Every tax they paid to Rome reminded them who ruled them."

**9. a06.** "To many of them, paying it felt like bowing to a foreign king in God's own land. … If He said no, He was calling for revolt, and those soldiers were watching."
**Verdict: correct.**
- **The tax as submission:** Judas the Galilean (*Jewish War* 2.118; Acts 5:37).
- **Caesar as "king":** the chief priests use the word for him (John 19:15).
- **The trap:** its aim was "to hand him over to the power and authority of the governor" (Luke 20:20). The charge was later made (Luke 23:2).

**Corrected:** no change.

**10. a07.** "Jesus knew their hypocrisy. 'Why are you trying to trap me?' He asked. 'Bring me a denarius and let me look at it.'"
**Verdict: correct.** Mark 12:15, NIV 2011.
**Corrected:** no change.

**11. a07.** "One of them took a silver denarius from his own purse and put it in Jesus' hand."
**Verdict: soften (minor).** Mark 12:16 says only "They brought the coin", and Matthew 22:19 "They brought him a denarius". The text doesn't say whose purse it came from. "Silver" is right. "Jesus held it up" (a10) is harmless staging.
**Corrected:** "One of them brought a silver denarius and put it in Jesus' hand."

**12. a08.** "On one side was a man's face, the emperor Tiberius."
**Verdict: correct, but traditional rather than certain.** The Gospels do not describe the coin. Tiberius was the reigning Caesar and his denarius was struck in huge numbers throughout his reign, so it is the most likely coin. But denarii of Augustus were in circulation too. Nothing in the theology depends on it, because Augustus' denarii make the same kind of claim: CAESAR AVGVSTVS DIVI F, "son of the divine [Julius]".
**Corrected (optional):** "On one side was a man's face, most likely the emperor Tiberius."

**13. a08.** "Around the face ran a claim. Tiberius Caesar, son of the divine Augustus. Augustus was the emperor before Tiberius, and Rome had declared him a god."
**Verdict: correct.** TI CAESAR DIVI AVG F AVGVSTVS means "Tiberius Caesar Augustus, son of the divine Augustus". It is fine for the ear to leave out Tiberius' own title, Augustus, at the end. The Senate declared Augustus a god on 17 September AD 14. If drawing a08 letters the legend, it must read TI CAESAR DIVI AVG F AVGVSTVS, with V, not U.
**Corrected:** no change. Optional fuller form: "Tiberius Caesar Augustus, son of the divine Augustus."

**14. a09.** "On the other side, the coin called Tiberius Pontifex Maximus, the highest priest. In one small piece of silver, a man claimed to be the son of a god, and the one who stood between his people and heaven."
**Verdict: correct.** PONTIF MAXIM is Pontifex Maximus, the head of Rome's state religion, whose duty was to keep the peace between Rome and its gods. "Stood between his people and heaven" is a fair gloss. The script rightly says "the son of a god", not "a god": Tiberius refused divine honours for himself (Suetonius, *Tiberius* 26; Tacitus, *Annals* 4.37–38).
**Corrected:** no change. (There is a dating fix for the story bible at the end.)

**15. a10.** "'Whose image is this?' He asked them. 'And whose inscription?' / 'Caesar's,' they replied. / Then Jesus said to them, 'Give back to Caesar what is Caesar's and to God what is God's.'"
**Verdict: correct.** Mark 12:16–17, NIV 2011. "Image" and "Give back" are the 2011 wording; the 1984 NIV had "portrait" and "Give".
**Corrected:** no change.

**16. a11.** "And they were amazed at Him. They left Him and went away."
**Verdict: correct.** The first sentence is Mark 12:17. The second comes from Matthew 22:22 ("So they left him and went away"), told as narration, which is fine.
**Corrected:** no change.

### 2. What belongs to God?

**17. p01.** "But Jesus had not stopped at Caesar. 'And to God what is God's.'" The drawing's words are "and to God what is God's?"
**Verdict: narration correct; drawing needs a minor fix.** On screen, the quotation marks plus a question mark change the NIV text. Letter the words exactly as Scripture has them, and let the large question mark that the cue already calls for carry the question.
**Corrected (drawing):** "…and to God what is God's", with the large question mark standing on its own.

**18. p02.** "The men who heard Him knew the answer, and I believe that is what amazed them."
**Verdict: soften.** This is the episode's key interpretive step. Linking "image" here to Genesis 1:26–27 is an ancient and sound reading (Tertullian, *On Idolatry* 15; *Against Marcion* 4.38). But Mark does not say the questioners made the link, and most commentators take their amazement to be at how Jesus escaped the trap. Let "I believe" cover both claims.
**Corrected:** "I believe the men who heard Him knew the answer, and that is what amazed them."

### 3. A word that lost its weight

**19. c01.** "The time has come. The kingdom of God has come near. Repent and believe the good news!"
**Verdict: correct.** Mark 1:15, NIV 2011 ("has come near"; the 1984 NIV had "is near"). "When Jesus first began to preach in Galilee" matches Mark 1:14.
**Corrected:** no change.

**20. c01.** "Nobody in that crowd needed the word kingdom explained. They were living inside one, under Rome."
**Verdict: soften.** Rome did not rule Galilee directly. It was the territory of Herod Antipas (4 BC to AD 39), Rome's client ruler, whom Mark calls "King Herod" (Mark 6:14). The change also connects to the Herodians in section 1.
**Corrected:** "Nobody in that crowd needed the word kingdom explained. They were living inside one. Herod ruled Galilee, and Rome ruled Herod."

**21. c03.** "Your kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven."
**Verdict: correct.** Matthew 6:10, NIV 2011. In j01, "Your kingdom come. Your will be done." keeps the same words, and the full stops suit the ear.
**Corrected:** no change.

**22. c04.** "Listen to the first two lines again."
**Verdict: fix (small).** These are not the first two lines of the Lord's Prayer, which opens "Our Father in heaven, hallowed be your name" (Matthew 6:9). A listener who knows the prayer will hear a mistake.
**Corrected:** "Listen to those two lines again."

**23. c04.** "They ask for the same thing twice."
**Verdict: soften (optional).** The two requests run in parallel and explain each other, as the author teaches ("The Kingdom comes when the will is done"), and most commentators agree. "The same thing" flattens them slightly, because "your kingdom come" also looks ahead to the end of the age.
**Corrected:** "They ask for one thing, in two ways."

**24. c04.** "A kingdom was the place where the king's will was done."
**Verdict: soften.** The Greek *basileia* (Aramaic *malkuta*) means first a king's reign and only then his realm. "The place" keeps only the second meaning. "Wherever" keeps both, and it still leads into the borders of section 4.
**Corrected:** "A kingdom was wherever the king's will was done."

### 4. The edge of a kingdom

**25. d01.** "In the world of the Bible, a king's land had edges, and stones marked them. Some of those stones survive today, still carrying the names of the kings who set them up."
**Verdict: soften (light) here, and fix the source in the story bible.**
- **True of border stelae.** Senusret III's boundary stelae at Semna marked Egypt's southern frontier (c. 1870 BC; "Southern boundary, made in year 8 under the Majesty of King Khakaure…"). Adad-nirari III set up the Pazarcık stele (805 BC, between Kummuh and Gurgum) and the Antakya stele (between Hamath and Arpad). Each carries the name of the king who set it up.
- **"Stones marked them" says too much.** Most frontiers were marked by rivers, hills and forts.
- **Not true of kudurrus.** Kudurrus recorded grants of land to officials and temples, and they were set up in temples, not at a kingdom's edge. Each names the king who made the grant, but it was usually commissioned by the man who received the land, so the king did not "set it up". Drop kudurrus as the source for d01–d02. Keep them for h02, where their curses on anyone who damages them are relevant.

**Corrected:** "In the world of the Bible, a king's land had edges, and kings set up stones to mark them. Some of those stones survive today, still carrying the kings' names."

**26. d02.** "Everyone who passed a stone like that knew what it meant. … One step past that stone, and the traveller is under a different king."
**Verdict: correct** as an illustration. The Semna stele forbids any Nubian to pass it heading north, except to trade. For a biblical anchor: Laban and Jacob set up a boundary pillar, and each promises not to "go past" it (Genesis 31:51–52).
**Corrected:** no change.

### 5. The farmer who forgot his king

**27. e01–e06.** The Assyrian farmer: "A share of everything he has grown belongs to the throne"; "every household owes him labour"; "one spring an enemy army gathers at the border".
**Verdict: correct** as a composite.
- **Taxes:** a grain tax (*nusāhē*) and a straw tax (*šibšu*), assessed on the harvest.
- **Labour:** service on royal works such as city walls (*ilku*, *tupšikku*).
- **War:** military service, and campaigns began in spring (2 Samuel 11:1). The source for the taxes, labour and service is J. N. Postgate, *Taxation and Conscription in the Assyrian Empire* (1974).
- **"Every household":** some cities and favoured estates were exempt, so this generalises, but a composite can carry it.
- **"In that ground":** Mesopotamian families often buried their dead under the house floor, so this fits.
- **A strong extra source:** 1 Samuel 8:11–17 describes this very life: "He will take your sons… He will take a tenth of your grain".
- **A theology note:** in Scripture that list is a warning about human kings, and Assyria is the great oppressor (Isaiah 10:5–14). v3 carries over to God only "held under the king" and the king's protection, not the taking. Keep it that way.

**Corrected:** no change.

**28. e08.** "Most of us live in democracies, where power starts with the people."
**Verdict: soften (optional).** The channel's audience is worldwide, and by the usual measures only about half the world's people live in any kind of democracy.
**Corrected:** "Many of us live in democracies, where power starts with the people."

**29. e08.** "In the farmer's world, power ran the other way. It came down from the throne."
**Verdict: correct.** Ancient Near Eastern kingship was said to come down from heaven. The Sumerian King List opens "After kingship had descended from heaven…"
**Corrected:** no change.

**30. e09.** "The earth is the LORD's, and everything in it," says the psalm, "the world, and all who live in it."
**Verdict: correct.** Psalm 24:1, NIV 2011. "The Bible was written in the farmer's world" is true in the sense the script means: every book of the Bible was written under kings or emperors.
**Corrected:** no change.

### 6. The king's face

**31. f01.** "When an Assyrian king brought a far-off land under his rule, he had his own likeness carved in stone. He set it up where the people of that land would pass it day after day."
**Verdict: correct.** Assyrian annals repeat the formula "I made an image of my royalty… and set it up". Examples:
- Tiglath-pileser III placed his image in the palace at Gaza.
- Sargon II set up stelae at Kition and at Ashdod (Isaiah 20:1).
- Esarhaddon's stele at Zincirli (Sam'al) stood, I believe, at the citadel gate. I'm unsure of the exact spot where it was found.
- Assyrian reliefs are carved beside the coast road at Nahr el-Kalb, near Beirut.

"Day after day" fits the gate stelae and the roadside reliefs.
**Corrected:** no change.

**32. f02.** "One of those stones was found on the island of Cyprus, far across the sea from the king's palace. It still carries his carved likeness."
**Verdict: correct.** This is Sargon II's basalt stele from Kition (modern Larnaca), c. 707 BC, found in 1845. It shows Sargon standing in relief, with the symbols of his gods. Its inscription tells how seven kings of Ia', a district of Yadnana (Cyprus), submitted and sent gifts.
- **Where it is now:** Berlin, in the Vorderasiatisches Museum. I believe the inventory number is VA 968, but I'm not sure of it. The museum's galleries are in the Pergamon Museum building, which closed for renovation in October 2023, so don't tell viewers the stele is on display.
- **Naming him:** if you want to, he is the Sargon of Isaiah 20:1, the only verse in the Bible that names him.

**Corrected:** no change.

**33. f02.** "The king himself was far away, and the people of that island knew what his image meant. This place was his now, and so were the people in it."
**Verdict: soften.** Assyria never annexed Cyprus. Its kings kept their thrones and sent tribute; around 673 BC Esarhaddon still lists ten Cypriot kings as his vassals. So "This place was his now" is what the image claimed, not the island's political reality. We also cannot know what the islanders made of it.
**Corrected:** "The king himself was far away, but his image made his claim for him. This place was his now, and so were the people in it."

**34. f03.** "Later, rulers found a way to send their face even further. They stamped it on coins…"
**Verdict: correct.**
**Corrected:** no change. (There is a dating fix for the story bible at the end.)

**35. f04.** "Caesar's face had travelled across his empire into the courts of God's house." … "the great King over all the earth."
**Verdict: correct.** Tiberius' silver coins were struck at Lugdunum (Lyon), so "across his empire" is exact. The second quotation is Psalm 47:2, NIV 2011.
**Corrected:** no change.

### 7. The first page

**36. g01.** "Now go back to the first page of the Bible, the page every man in those courts had learned as a boy."
**Verdict: soften.** Not every man in the courts was a Jew brought up on the Law. Greeks came up to worship at the festival (John 12:20), and Roman soldiers stood along the colonnades, as the script has just said. The rest is sound: Jewish boys were taught the Law from childhood, mostly by hearing it read (Josephus, *Against Apion* 2.175, 178).
**Corrected:** "Now go back to the first page of the Bible, the page the men in those courts had learned as boys."

**37. g01.** "God speaks, the way a king speaks, and what He says simply happens. 'And God said, "Let there be light," and there was light.'"
**Verdict: correct.** Genesis 1:3, NIV 2011. For the power of a king's word, see Psalm 33:9 and Ecclesiastes 8:4 ("Since a king's word is supreme…").
**Corrected:** no change.

**38. g02.** "Day by day He fills His world. … All of it is His, because He made it."
**Verdict: correct.** It summarises Genesis 1:9–25. See also Psalm 24:1–2 and Psalm 95:5 ("The sea is his, for he made it").
**Corrected:** no change.

**39. g03.** "And then, last of all, He does something every person in that ancient world would have understood. He places His image in His land."
**Verdict: soften "every person"; the theology is correct.**
- **Mainstream reading.** Reading the image of God as a king's image placed in his realm is mainstream Old Testament scholarship (D. J. A. Clines, 1968; J. R. Middleton, *The Liberating Image*, 2005). It fits "so that they may rule".
- **Ancient parallels.** Egyptian and Assyrian texts call the king the "image" of a god. The 9th-century BC Tell Fekheriye statue calls a king's statue his "image" and his "likeness", using the Aramaic forms of the same two words as Genesis 1:26.
- **What Genesis changes.** It gives that royal title to every human being, male and female.

**Corrected:** "And then, last of all, He does something the people of that ancient world would have understood."

**40. g03.** "Then God said, 'Let us make mankind in our image, in our likeness, so that they may rule…' So God created mankind in his own image, in the image of God he created them."
**Verdict: correct.** Genesis 1:26–27, NIV 2011 ("mankind", "so that they may rule"). The narrator should pause at the ellipsis so the listener hears the cut.
**Corrected:** no change.

**41. b02.** "Every man in those courts knew the answer from the first page of his Scriptures."
**Verdict: soften**, for the reasons in notes 18 and 36.
**Corrected:** "The men in those courts had the answer on the first page of their Scriptures."

**42. g04.** "God's image is alive. You are the statue that breathes, and the coin that walks."
**Verdict: correct**, and well grounded. The Hebrew word for "image", *tselem*, is also used for a statue or idol (2 Kings 11:18; Daniel 3:1). Idols "have no breath in them" (Jeremiah 10:14; Psalm 135:17; Habakkuk 2:19), but God breathed life into the man (Genesis 2:7).
**Corrected:** no change.

**43. b03.** "The tax question was small. The ownership question was total. It means you, every part of you, on every day of the week."
**Verdict: correct.** This is sound and not overclaimed. The next exchange in Mark is "Love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind and with all your strength" (Mark 12:30). See also Psalm 24:1, Romans 12:1 and 1 Corinthians 6:19–20 ("You are not your own"). Once note 18 is applied, the listener has already heard the image reading framed as the author's own.
**Corrected:** no change.

### 8. What the image gives you

**44. h01.** "A newborn baby carries the King's image in full."
**Verdict: soften.** The script is right that every human bears God's image, after the fall as well as before (Genesis 9:6; James 3:9). It is also right that the newborn, the grandmother and the prisoner bear it equally. But "in full" can be heard as "unmarred". The New Testament reserves the full image for Christ (Colossians 1:15; 2 Corinthians 4:4; Hebrews 1:3) and speaks of believers being renewed into it (Colossians 3:10; 2 Corinthians 3:18).
**Corrected:** "A newborn baby carries the King's image as truly as anyone."

**45. h02.** "In the ancient world, how you treated a king's image was how you treated the king himself."
**Verdict: correct**, and there is a source from Tiberius' own reign. Under his treason law it became a capital charge to beat a slave near a statue of Augustus, or to carry a coin or ring bearing his image into a latrine or a brothel (Suetonius, *Tiberius* 58). Assyrian and Babylonian stelae curse anyone who damages them. In the Bible, see Genesis 9:6 and James 3:9 ("we curse human beings, who have been made in God's likeness").
**Corrected:** no change.

### 9–10. The question comes home; Next time

**46. j01.** "…pray over it the words Jesus taught. 'Your kingdom come. Your will be done.'"
**Verdict: correct.** Matthew 6:10.
**Corrected:** no change.

**47. k01.** "Then you will hear God say, 'I will be your God and you will be my people,' the way the first hearers heard it."
**Verdict: correct.**
- **The quotation** is Jeremiah 7:23, NIV 2011, word for word ("Obey me, and I will be your God and you will be my people"). The verse recalls the command God gave at the exodus (7:22).
- **The reference.** Exodus 6:7 ("I will take you as my own people, and I will be your God") and Jeremiah 30:22 ("So you will be my people, and I will be your God") don't match the line, so keep the reference as Jeremiah 7:23. If episode 2 wants the form given at Sinai, Leviticus 26:12 reads "I will walk among you and be your God, and you will be my people."
- **The treaty description.** "The kind of document a great king used to bind a people to himself" is also right. Hittite treaties open "Thus says … the Great King", and the Bible quotes the Assyrian title, "the great king, the king of Assyria" (2 Kings 18:19, 28).

**Corrected:** no change.

## Requested checks that do not apply to v3's narration

- **"King of kings":** not in the script. If it is added: Assyrian kings used it from Tukulti-Ninurta I (13th century BC) onwards. The Bible gives it to Nebuchadnezzar (Ezekiel 26:7; Daniel 2:37), to Artaxerxes (Ezra 7:12), and to God and Christ (1 Timothy 6:15; Revelation 17:14; 19:16). For this series, "the Great King" is the closer title (Psalm 47:2; Psalm 48:2 and Matthew 5:35, "the city of the Great King").
- **The Antonia:** not named. If it is named: Herod the Great rebuilt it and named it after Mark Antony. It stood at the north-west corner of the temple courts.
- **Kudurru:** not named in the narration. See note 25.
- **Luke 17:21:** not quoted in v3. If it is used later, the NIV 2011 reads "the kingdom of God is in your midst" (the 1984 NIV had "within you").

## Story bible corrections

- **a03:** add Matthew 26:55 as the source for "sat teaching".
- **a05:** add *Antiquities* 18.90–95, which shows the Romans held the Antonia from AD 6 to 36. Whiston's "legion" in *Jewish War* 5.244 is a cohort.
- **a08–a09:** change "Struck AD 14–37" to "AD 15–37". The coin calls Tiberius pontifex maximus, and he took that office on 10 March AD 15. Also note that the coin's identification is traditional and probable, not certain.
- **d01–d02:** drop kudurrus as the source. Cite Senusret III's Semna stelae and Adad-nirari III's Antakya stele alongside the Pazarcık stele.
- **e01–e06:** add 1 Samuel 8:11–17.
- **f01–f02:** say where the Kition stele is now: Berlin, in the Vorderasiatisches Museum (VA 968, I believe). It has probably been off view since October 2023.
- **f03:** early coins (Lydia, c. 600 BC) had no portraits. Rulers' portraits first appear on coins of Persian satraps in the late 5th century BC and become the norm under Alexander's successors around 300 BC.
- **h02:** add Suetonius, *Tiberius* 58; Genesis 9:6; James 3:9.
